FedCast: the brief

A forecaster for the next Federal Reserve rate decision, built to be judged by its process rather than its luck.

Status as of 28 October 2026, decision day · repository kmt112/fed-forecast · ledger entry #18 · spec amendment #7

The thesis

A large language model inside an engineered harness produces forecasts that are measurably more repeatable and more faithful to evidence than the same model asked naively. The way to prove that is not to wait for the forecasts to come true (eight decisions a year is far too few, and the market is already very good at this) but to measure the process: does the same question get the same answer, can every claim be traced to evidence, does any number come from memory, does a shock to one input move the answer the right way, does an irrelevant change leave it alone, does human input flow only through channels that can be audited.

Two things are worth the room's time. The first is how the language model is harnessed and scored, because that is the part nobody else shows and the part that makes an AI's output repeatable. The second is the blended model, because every number in it can be taken apart and traced to its evidence. Sections 1 and 2 are those two things; the rest is the record.

1. Harnessing the AI, and scoring it

The idea in one paragraph. A language model is treated as an instrument, not an oracle. Code fixes what it is allowed to see, fixes the shape of what it must return, checks what it returned against the evidence, runs it several times to see whether it gives the same answer, and records all of it. Only after those checks pass is the model allowed to influence the number, and even then only by a bounded amount. The checks are written down as a scorecard before any result is seen, and the same scorecard is applied to a bare, unharnessed model so the value of the harness is measured rather than asserted.

1.1 Three sets of names, and how they fit together

The project uses three vocabularies that are easy to mix up. They answer three different questions.

Analysts 1 to 8: who is run.

Eight configurations of the same model (Claude) on the same frozen evidence. They differ only in what they are shown and what they must return. Analysts 1 to 3 are the whole-evidence readers; 4 to 8 are specialists, one per slice of evidence.

Arms A to D: the experiment.

An arm is one rung of a ladder. Each rung adds exactly one technique to the rung below, so the difference in score between neighbours is the value of that technique. A is analyst 1 with no harness; B is analyst 2 with the evidence added; C is analyst 3 with the answer shape and the code verifier added; D is analysts 4 to 8 with specialisation added. A and B are controls, expected to fail.

S1 to S10: the yardstick.

Ten questions with pass marks, fixed on 21 September before any analyst existed. S1, S2, S3, S8 and S10 are about the model's behaviour and are measured from the analysts' recorded runs. S4 to S7 are about the whole chain and are measured by code perturbing the evidence. S9 is about the record.

The harness: what sits around the model.

Five mechanisms, listed in 1.2. Arms B, C and D are those mechanisms switched on one group at a time; the scorecard is how we know each one did something.

A pre-read is a number computed by code from the evidence and handed to the analyst as text, for example the inflation gap or the statement's word diff. 1.2 The harness: five mechanisms around every analyst

  1. Evidence by construction. The prompt is built by code from one frozen, fingerprinted snapshot of evidence. The model runs with every tool disabled: it cannot browse, fetch or read files. It sees what we chose and nothing else.
  2. Shape by schema. The answer must match a fixed structure: claims with verbatim quotes and item ids, a report on each standing watch-out, a tilt between −10 and +10 basis points, a confidence, a short summary. Anything else is rejected before anyone reads it.
  3. Verification by code. Each quote is matched character for character against the named item (a quote stitched from two places fails). Each number in a claim is looked for in the whole evidence set. Each watch-out must be answered by id. The tilt is clipped. The scores come from these checks, not from the model's own confidence.
  4. Recording. Every call is stored under a hash of its prompt, with the full output. Any run can be replayed exactly; nothing can be edited without a visible commit.
  5. Scoring. Five identical runs per analyst give repeatability; the verifier gives groundedness and leakage; the watch-out check gives discipline. These are S1, S2, S3 and S10. An analyst earns influence on the number only by passing them, and only by a logged amendment.

1.5 Why this is the thing to show

Anyone can call a language model and get an opinion about the Fed. The question a desk, an auditor or a model-risk committee asks is different: when is it safe to let that opinion touch a number, and by how much? FedCast answers with measurements rather than assurances. The bare model fails three of four tests; the harnessed model passes all four on three snapshots in a row; so the harnessed model is allowed to lean the number, by at most ten points, through the same bounded mechanism a human view uses, and the number before its lean is recorded beside the number after. Every part of that sentence is a figure in the app.

2.1 How a model earns a place, and how the weights are set

Two separate questions, deliberately kept apart.

Whether a model is in the pool is decided by a backtest with one fixed design: for every scheduled decision from January 2010 on, fit on all decisions before it and predict it (an expanding window), using data exactly as it was first published. The score is the log score, which rewards calibrated probabilities and punishes confident misses. A model joins only if it beats the base rate that needs no fitting at all.

What weight a model gets is not fitted on that backtest, on purpose: fitting weights on the same history the models were built from would tune on the past and tell us nothing new. The weights (market 0.6, Taylor 0.2, odds 0.1, base rates 0.1) were set by reasoning, logged as amendments, and are now scored on live decisions from today onward, each track, each model and two naive baselines, into an append-only scores file. A different weight set is adopted only when that record says so, and only by amendment. The market is the anchor because at a five-week horizon it already embeds everything, including every rule.

2.3 What moves the number, and by how much

The S4 shocks double as a sensitivity table. On the 24 October snapshot, 10 bp of extra tightening priced into the futures moved the pool's expected change by +6.9 bp; half a point more unemployment moved it −1.9 bp; a strong hawkish view moved exactly 10.00 points of probability and no more. The five noise changes (S5) moved nothing. Those two tables, shown in the Scorecard view for any snapshot, are the answer to "what would change your mind, and what would not".

2.4 Why the rules get 0.2 and not the whole number

A common objection is that the rate follows a rule (a Taylor rule, or the rule inside a model such as the New York Fed's DSGE), so forecasting the meeting is pointless. We measured it on 206 scheduled decisions from 2000 to 2026 with the Fed's own four published rules and the vintages known the day before each meeting. The actual rate was within half a point of the rules' rate at 16% of meetings and more than a full point away at 62%; in early 2022 the rules wanted about 8% while the committee held at 0.25%. On direction, the rules agreed with the decision at 35% of meetings, against 68% for always predicting no change. The rules describe a tendency with unobservable inputs; the committee decides. That is why the Taylor family is one voice at 0.2, the market is the anchor, and the one thing borrowed from the DSGE is its estimate of the natural rate (amendment #7), not its forecast.

2.5 Every number replays

Each ledger entry names its snapshot, its spec hash, its code version and the analyst runs it used. Recomputing it must give back the published probabilities (S9). The spec hash covers the scorecard, the weights, the floor, the tilt rules and every model constant that can move the number, and it is pinned in the test suite, so a silent change breaks the build. Changes are numbered amendments with reasons, #0 to #7 so far. Two findings from running this check are worth telling: amendment #4 once changed a model rule without a new hash and four entries stopped replaying, which amendment #5 closed by putting every rule inside the hash; and on 28 October the replay was found to read the latest analyst runs rather than the runs the entry had used, so a rerun of the analysts would have broken replay of an honest entry. It now reads the runs the entry names.

5. How to read the app in two minutes

  1. Top bar: when the evidence was last pulled, and what is new at the sources since.
  2. Forecast: the machine number and the number after your views, side by side.
  3. Evidence graph: click any box. Blue is in the number; grey is computed but not in it; dotted is planned. Click a model to see only what it uses.
  4. Analyst prompts: what each of the eight analysts is asked and shown, down to the full prompt. If it is not on that page, the analyst does not get it.
  5. Analyst results: what each concluded on this snapshot, every claim with its quote and a verified or not-found mark.
  6. Scorecard: the yardstick; the measurements on any snapshot you pick (hover a cell for what was counted); every recorded run; and the over-time table that shows whether anything has drifted.
  7. Ledger: every entry, its replay status, and the Score button once a decision is in the evidence.
